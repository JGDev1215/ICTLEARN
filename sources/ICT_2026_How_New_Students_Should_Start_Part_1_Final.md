## 1. Central lesson: learn to read price before seeking entries

**Editorial synthesis of the teaching sequence:**

> Prepare → map higher-timeframe references → identify plausible destinations → specify expected behaviour and invalidation → observe delivery → reassess before considering execution.

The initial task is to understand **where price could go, why, which intermediate levels matter, and what would change that expectation**. ICT explicitly distinguishes this navigation exercise from a trade entry. **[T 53:43–55:04; 61:49–62:01; 106:18–106:24]**

The screenshots summarise this as combining time, price, liquidity, PD arrays, range structure and narrative—not treating one gap or pattern as sufficient. This is the note author’s synthesis rather than a verbatim lecture formula. **[I3]**

## 2. Economic calendar: a timing filter

Prepare before the trading week. Filter the calendar for the market being studied: USD events in this example; relevant currencies when studying forex. Focus on medium- and high-impact events and record their **day and release time**. ICT uses these to anticipate potential disruption to expected price delivery, rather than to forecast the released number. **[T 1:13–2:22; 5:00–5:20; 6:21–6:27; 10:35–11:02]**

**Beginner guidance for the NFP week discussed:** study Monday for opportunities without treating every Monday as compulsory; on Tuesday, allow the 10:00 release to occur before working with subsequent price action; finish Wednesday by 11:00; use Thursday and Friday for observation rather than trading. These are the lecturer’s instructional filters, not demonstrated weekday probabilities. **[T 3:44–4:29; 8:31–9:20]**

## 3. Higher-timeframe preparation and chart organisation

**Monthly references.** Mark the previous completed month’s high, low and close. July is completed in the example; August is still trading, so its displayed high/low are provisional and its closing price is not yet available. ICT omits the monthly open here and prioritises New Week Opening Gaps. His explanation should not be converted into a universal rule that monthly and weekly openings coincide. **[T 11:34–12:02; 14:22–15:15; 15:47–16:49]**

**Range-relative location.** Identify the range being measured before calling price premium or discount. Above its midpoint is premium; below is discount. The same price can be in July’s upper half while slightly below August’s midpoint. **[T 22:45–23:38]**

**Carry context downward.** The walkthrough moves from monthly to a weekly view, then daily, hourly, 15-minute, 5-minute and 1-minute charts. Keep significant higher-timeframe annotations visible on lower timeframes; right-justify their labels so their meaning remains visible. **[T 13:32–14:17; 16:42–16:50; 21:03–21:23; 26:17–27:35; 34:18–34:22; 36:48–37:05]**

**Separate layouts.** Maintain accessible views for higher-timeframe references, opening gaps and active observation. ICT discusses New Week Opening Gaps, New Day Opening Gaps, first presented fair value gaps and RTH opening gaps. The aim is access without placing every annotation on one chart. **[T 18:14–20:30; 43:37–45:12]**

## 4. Daily structures and range subdivisions

Mark relevant daily imbalances and other reference structures near current price, then study how lower-timeframe candles interact with them. The examples include BISI, SIBI, volume imbalances and a Suspension Block. In the illustrated case, ICT describes a SIBI with volume imbalances at both boundaries as a Suspension Block. **[T 27:43–28:38; 30:15–31:59; 33:38–34:08]**

Grade the chosen range into **halves, quarters and eighths**. Equilibrium is the midpoint; octants divide the range into eight equal parts. Record the anchors and measurement direction: the illustrated `0.125` line is the first subdivision **down from the high**, not automatically a level near the low. **[T 28:38–29:59; 39:57–40:10; 46:24–46:43; I5]**

For anchors `H` and `L`, the midpoint is `(H + L) / 2`. These are reference coordinates for observation—not evidence that price must react there.

## 5. Build a draw on liquidity, not a fixed prediction

A **draw on liquidity (DOL)** is the potential destination being considered. ICT treats highs and lows as possible locations for stop or breakout orders and also considers inefficiencies and opening-gap references. The presence and quantity of actual resting orders are not established by the chart discussion. **[T 24:35–25:10; 39:27–39:55; 44:14–44:35]**

In the bearish example, he first watches the next graded daily level; only if delivery continues does the lower sell-side objective become the next focus. A close above the identified opposing reference would change the narrative. **[T 53:43–55:04]**

**Competing destinations can coexist.** Before the RTH open, the lecture contrasts lower sell-side liquidity with a move towards the previous RTH closing reference. Beginners are told to observe which route price supports rather than claim certainty. **[T 95:18–98:07]**

## 6. Observe continuation, warning signs and narrative changes

**Wick midpoint / consequent encroachment.** Measure the selected wick—not automatically the whole candle. In the short example, ICT prefers retracement bodies to remain below the relevant wick midpoint. A close above a watched reference creates concern and prompts reassessment; it is not universally an instruction to exit immediately. **[T 48:43–49:23; 55:51–56:09; 59:03–60:04; 71:25–71:36]**

**Fair value gap versus inversion.** In the example, rejection from a bearish gap supports the bearish narrative. Trading above it and then treating it as support on a return would support the alternative bullish interpretation. ICT uses the distinction to assess continuation or a possible intermediate-term turn. **[T 76:23–77:18; 80:58–81:38]**

**Nested references.** A gap overlapping a wick’s midpoint gives two nearby PD-array references for assessing the same price interaction. This is additional feedback within the framework, not a quantified probability increase. **[T 79:00–79:21]**

**Immediate rebalance.** In the later example, ICT checks candle prices, states that no volume imbalance or FVG is present in the specific interaction, and labels it immediate rebalance. Do not relabel every such interaction as an FVG. **[T 116:31–117:19]**

## 7. Macro timing: when to assess delivery, not which direction to trade

The macro described here spans the **last 10 minutes of an hour and the first 10 minutes of the next hour**, for example 08:50–09:10. It supplies no directional bias; direction comes from the preceding analysis. **[T 63:09–63:15; 65:09–65:34; 90:14–90:30]**

ICT expects the proposed move to become evident during that window. Prolonged consolidation instead of expected continuation weakens the narrative. He also discusses movement immediately after the window, so its end is **not an automatic reversal signal or rigid invalidation rule**. **[T 79:53–80:00; 82:35–83:15; 89:35–90:36]**

## 8. RTH opening gap: anchors, midpoint and the 70% claim

Let `S` be the previous RTH closing reference, called “settlement” in the lecture, and `O` the 09:30 opening price. The excerpt does not establish an exchange settlement calculation; retain the chart reference actually described. **[T 41:41–43:19; 51:28–51:45]**

| Opening relationship | Lecture classification | Gap low | Gap high |
|---|---|---|---|
| `O > S` | Premium / gap higher | `S` | `O` |
| `O < S` | Discount / gap lower | `O` | `S` |

The gap midpoint is `(O + S) / 2`; full closure means returning to `S`. Do not confuse this **price interval** with the **09:30–10:00 opening-range time window**. **[T 42:53–43:19; 51:02–51:58; 93:34–93:38; 95:23–95:29; 97:16–97:41]**

**Attributed statistical claim:** ICT states that, when an opening gap exists, half the gap is filled before 10:00 approximately **70% of the time**. The supplied material establishes that he said this; it supplies no sample size, test period, gap-size filter or reproducible methodology. It is **not** a 70% trade win rate or a 70% full-gap-fill claim. **[T 97:21–97:41]**

**Anchor caution:** he subsequently discusses halving a range from a later low to the previous closing reference. That uses different anchors; do not silently substitute it for the opening-to-closing-reference midpoint above. **[T 97:45–98:07]**

## 9. Hypothetical management is secondary to observation

**Event horizon.** In the bearish example, ICT measures halfway between a nearby low and the next lower target as a possible partial-profit area. This is not defined as halfway between entry and stop, nor as a fixed reward-to-risk multiple. **[T 68:38–70:54; 77:54–78:03]**

He discusses taking partials and tightening a stop as continuation becomes less convincing. These are hypothetical tape-reading examples, not verified executions or a universal allocation rule. Do not infer that taking a partial necessarily covers the remaining risk and costs. **[T 71:53–72:25; 86:15–86:45; 88:36–88:49; 90:37–91:04]**

## 10. Practice and journal the decision before the outcome

ICT recommends observing without a position, reviewing recorded one-minute price action, and pausing to state what should or should not happen before continuing. His suggested review window is **09:30–10:30**. Screenshot decision points, record the expectation, then document subsequent behaviour. The objective is to measure the approach through personal observation, not assume it works. **[T 73:10–74:18; 80:13–80:28; 107:56–108:54; 115:01–116:25]**

**Editorial journal template derived from those instructions:**

```text
Session / instrument / contract / New York time:
Higher-timeframe range anchors and current position:
Candidate destinations and reason for the preferred one:
Relevant PD arrays and exact reference prices:
Expected behaviour and timing — recorded before the outcome:
What would weaken or invalidate the expectation:
What actually happened; continuation, rejection or no delivery:
Before/after screenshots and lesson:
```
